## 1. Identify the subject of protection

| Concept | Lecture distinction | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copyright | Original expression, including specific code and content; not the underlying idea or method | IP pp. 4, 7 |
| Trade mark | Identifying signs such as names, logos and other distinctive marks | IP pp. 13–14 |
| Patent | An eligible invention or functional innovation; claims define the protected scope | PAT pp. 4, 6–7 |
| Design rights | Visual appearance of physical products in the lecture's Australian framing | IP pp. 16–18 |

Different rights can apply to different aspects of one product. For example, an app's original code, brand and a qualifying technical invention raise different questions. The lecture explicitly allows multiple IP types rather than choosing exactly one. **[IP p. 18]**

Copyright does not require that nobody has ever written similar code: independent creation and copying are different. Copyright protection of code does not give ownership of every implementation of the same idea. A domain name, a trade mark and copyright in website content are also distinct. **[IP pp. 7, 11, 15]**

## 2. Licences: permission comes with terms

Open-source software still has copyright and licence conditions. Check the actual licence when using, modifying or distributing it. **[IP pp. 8–10]**

| Family | Core idea | Revision caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copyleft | Grant freedoms while requiring their preservation in covered derivative works | Check which works and distribution events trigger obligations |
| Permissive | Allow broad reuse with comparatively few conditions | Fewer restrictions does not mean no restrictions |

These are the distinctions on **IP p. 9**. Do not equate open source with public domain, or copyleft with a universal ban on commercial use: the slide's definition permits redistribution with or without a fee. The exact licence controls the conditions.

## 3. Patents: four requirements and three specification parts

The guest lecture asks whether an invention is **new, inventive, useful, and eligible subject matter**; it also mentions further legal requirements such as adequate disclosure. **[PAT p. 7]**

| Specification part | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Description | Explain how the invention works, including the best method known to the applicant at filing |
| Drawings | Clarify the described invention |
| Claims | Define the scope of the exclusive rights |

Source: **PAT p. 6**.

The patent system exchanges limited exclusive rights for disclosure, encouraging investment and follow-on innovation. A search addresses two separate questions: **can this invention be protected**, and **could it infringe someone else's rights**? **[PAT pp. 3–4, 10]**

The lecture distinguishes a **provisional application**, which establishes a priority date but does not provide patent protection, from a complete application. It presents PCT as a route that streamlines applications in multiple jurisdictions, not a single global patent. Filing an application should not be confused with a granted right. **[PAT pp. 11, 21]**

## 4. Computing inventions: substance matters

The lecture does not treat all software as categorically excluded. It asks whether the substance is an eligible invention rather than a scheme, abstract idea or mere information. **[PAT pp. 12–13]**

| More supportive indicators in the lecture | Weaker indicators |
| --- | --- |
| Solves a technical problem | Solves only a business problem |
| Improves computer operation | Uses an ordinary computer for its usual functions |
| Produces a technical effect | Merely processes information |
| Creates new technology | Automates an existing idea |

These are discussion indicators, not a guaranteed legal test. **[PAT pp. 19–20]**

In the **ROKT** case study, the key distinction is between a marketing scheme involving engagement offers and an improvement to computing technology. The lecture warns that “a computer has not done this before” is insufficient by itself. **[PAT pp. 15–18]**

## 5. Professional responsibility and codes

Professionalism combines competence, responsible judgement and obligations to society. Personal morals are individual beliefs; professional ethics supplies shared standards. A code guides judgement rather than automatically calculating an answer. **[E pp. 3–6; IP pp. 21–24]**

The ACS lecture uses **three values**:

| Value | Practical application |
| --- | --- |
| Honesty | Be truthful about evidence, defects, limitations and uncertainty |
| Trustworthiness | Accept accountability and act with integrity |
| Respect | Consider people, communities and the profession |

Source: **E p. 5**; applications are explanatory summaries.

The separate software-engineering code discussed in the IP deck covers **public, client/employer, product, judgement, management, profession, colleagues and self**. Its examples include documenting problems, protecting privacy/confidentiality, fair attribution and protecting people who raise ethical concerns. Keep this framework distinct from the ACS three values. **[IP pp. 25–27]**

## 6. Apply the ethics framework to a scenario

The lecture's process is: identify stakeholders → gather facts → assess harms → apply ACS values → act in the public interest. Ask whether the decision could be justified publicly. **[E p. 13]**

| Case | Issue emphasised | Organisational theme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizon | Software errors, ignored warnings and responsibility for harm | Governance and accountability |
| Social media | Engagement features, foreseeable risks and wellbeing | Culture and incentives |
| Robodebt | Accuracy concerns, warnings and failure to act | Leadership and decision-making |

Source: **E pp. 9–12**. These are lecture case summaries, not independent updates on litigation.

For an answer, connect a **specific fact** to a **specific affected group**, explain the harm, and justify a concrete response. For example, an original practice scenario involving a known data-loss defect could discuss documenting evidence, escalating the risk, correcting misleading assurances and protecting affected users. Merely naming “honesty” without applying it is incomplete reasoning.

Other lecture prompts include addictive design, data ownership/privacy, algorithmic bias, cybersecurity, copyright, environmental effects and fair credit. Technical success alone does not settle these questions. **[IP pp. 28–29; E pp. 7–8, 14]**
